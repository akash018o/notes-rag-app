Passwords and Authentication

Authentication is the process of proving who you are to a system. Authentication factors are usually grouped into three types: something you know, such as a password or PIN; something you have, such as a phone or hardware security key; and something you are, such as a fingerprint or face scan. Multi-factor authentication, or MFA, requires two or more of these different types, so stealing a password alone is not enough to get in.

Strong passwords are long rather than just complicated. A passphrase made of several random words is usually stronger and easier to remember than a short password full of symbols. Passwords should never be reused across sites, because when one site is breached, attackers try the leaked passwords on other services in an attack called credential stuffing. A password manager helps by generating and storing a unique password for every account.

Systems should never store passwords in plain text. Instead they store a hash, which is the output of a one-way function that cannot be reversed. When you log in, the system hashes what you typed and compares it to the stored hash. Attackers who steal a database of hashes try to guess passwords offline. To make this harder, systems add a random value called a salt to each password before hashing, so identical passwords produce different hashes. Slow, purpose-built algorithms such as bcrypt, scrypt, and Argon2 are recommended because they make each guess expensive. Fast general-purpose hashes like MD5 or SHA-1 are unsuitable for storing passwords.

Common password attacks include brute force, which tries every possible combination; dictionary attacks, which try common words and leaked passwords; and password spraying, which tries a few very common passwords against many accounts to avoid lockouts. Defences include account lockout or rate limiting, monitoring for unusual login attempts, and blocking passwords that are known to have appeared in breaches.

Not all MFA is equally strong. SMS codes can be intercepted through SIM swapping. Authenticator apps that generate time-based one-time passwords are better. Hardware security keys and passkeys are the strongest because they are resistant to phishing: they only work on the genuine website.
