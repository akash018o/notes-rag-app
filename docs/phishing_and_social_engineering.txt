Phishing and Social Engineering

Social engineering is the practice of manipulating people into giving up confidential information or performing actions that compromise security. Instead of breaking into a system with technical tricks, the attacker exploits human trust, fear, urgency, or curiosity. Social engineering is often the easiest way into an organization because people are usually easier to fool than well-configured software.

Phishing is the most common form of social engineering. In a phishing attack, the attacker sends a message, usually an email, that pretends to come from a trusted source such as a bank, a delivery company, or an employer. The message urges the victim to click a link, open an attachment, or enter their credentials on a fake website. Typical warning signs include a sender address that does not quite match the real company, generic greetings like "Dear customer", spelling mistakes, unexpected attachments, and pressure to act immediately.

Spear phishing is a targeted version of phishing. The attacker researches a specific person, using their name, job title, and colleagues, so the message looks believable. Whaling is spear phishing aimed at senior executives. Business email compromise is a related scam in which an attacker impersonates an executive or supplier and asks the finance team to transfer money or change bank details.

Other related attacks include smishing, which uses SMS text messages, and vishing, which uses voice calls. In vishing, a caller may pretend to be bank support or IT staff and ask for a one-time password. Pretexting is when the attacker invents a believable story, such as pretending to be a new employee who needs help getting access. Baiting leaves something tempting, such as an infected USB drive labeled "salaries", for the victim to pick up. Tailgating means following an authorized person through a secured door without using a badge.

How to defend against social engineering: Check the sender's real address and hover over links to see where they really go before clicking. Never share one-time passwords or passwords with anyone who contacts you first, even if they claim to be from IT or a bank. Report suspicious messages to the security team instead of just deleting them. Use multi-factor authentication so that a stolen password alone is not enough. Organizations should run regular awareness training and simulated phishing exercises.
